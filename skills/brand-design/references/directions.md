# Directions

A direction is a way the brand could go: one idea for what it's about, rendered as the look that idea produces. It runs in two rounds: a wide round of ten to twelve direction cards, each anchored on a reference, and a full round where the user's two or three picks get full boards on the real screen. Sources: fatihaydost/brand-identity-skill (mechanism, expression move, axes), Kappaemme's build-startup-brand (anatomy, scorecard, hybrid rule), Ragged Edge on Wise (routes, pick the idea), Michael Johnson (safe, adventurous, scary).

## Why two rounds

Studios start from 20 to 30 ideas and show three. Going straight to three full boards skips the wide part, and the first run showed what happens: three light grounds, one shared scheme, the same layout, and only the face and one accent telling them apart. The user's verdict: "not nearly broad enough." The wide round is cheap on purpose (one frame per idea), so breadth costs little and the user reacts to range before anything gets built properly.

## Why references lead

A direction built up from tokens lands on the average and looks hand-rolled. A direction that starts from a composition that's already great, with our content swapped in, starts at that craft level. So each card is anchored on one or two specific references (a real brand, a product screen, a poster, an object), shown beside it. In the wide round it's fine to lift a reference's palette, type genre, and composition nearly whole; the card's job is to show the user a look, not to be original yet. It becomes ours in the full board (the mechanism and our product's subject reshape it) and in the sprints. The clone risk lives in the final mark and tokens, and the reflex test and the line-up catch it there.

## Round 1: the wide round

Ten to twelve cards, each one frame on the brand file's "🚧 Directions" page, all the same size and layout so they compare.

**Forced spread.** Plan the set as a table before drawing any card, and check it against these floors:
- **Ground.** At least three dark grounds and at least two where a color is the ground (not an accent). No more than half the cards on white or off-white.
- **Hue.** At least four hue families across the set. No two cards share ground and accent.
- **Type genre.** No two cards in the same genre (grotesk, condensed, slab, serif, mono, rounded, display or novelty, pixel).
- **Texture.** At least three textures across the set: flat, print (halftone, risograph, letterpress, newsprint), photographic or material (card stock, foil, felt), pixel or broadcast.
- **Risk.** Label each safe, adventurous, or scary; at least three scary.

**One card.** In this order, top to bottom:
- Name (two or three words, from its idea, never a style word) and its label.
- The reference it's anchored on, as a thumbnail with its source, and one line on what it lifts.
- The hero frame: the wordmark in the candidate face, the product's main headline (a number or a line from its screens), and one product fragment (a card, a row, a chart corner) rendered in the look. Real product copy and numbers only.
- The palette as four to six chips with one-word roles, and the face named.

Cards are designed freely in Paper (or as HTML screenshots when Paper is down), not as style overrides on the live app. Overrides inherit the app's ground, layout, chart, and table, which caps how far a direction can move and is why the first run looked hand-rolled.

**Pick.** The user picks two or three, or names a blend (see hybrids). Ask for the word back; show the agent's lean and runner-up first.

## Round 2: the full board

Each pick gets a full board. Every field is written before anything is drawn.

- **Name.** From round 1.
- **Mechanism sentence.** One visual idea that yields the mark, the type, and the palette together. "A blueprint's grid: hairline-cut wordmark, grid-built symbol, white-on-blue palette." If the mark, type, and palette don't all fall out of it, it's a mood, not a mechanism.
- **Expression move.** The one loud thing, and only one: an oversized numeral, a single saturated color on one job, a cut through the wordmark. Everything else is quiet.
- **Borrowed object.** The physical thing or world it takes from (a boarding pass, a weather map, a seed packet). "SaaS" isn't an answer.
- **References.** The round 1 anchor plus one or two more, each with the trait borrowed.
- **Axes.** All boards placed at -2 to +2 on the same three to five of: light/dark ground, warm/cool, quiet/loud, friend/authority, classic/new, dense/airy, crafted/engineered. Every pair differs by 2 or more points on at least two of them.
- **Palette with roles.** Ground, neutral (with its tint), ink, accent and where it's allowed, sign colors if the product has them. Hex plus the job, never a mood word. A dark-ground direction proves its sign colors and chart tone on dark.
- **Type.** Display, text, and numerals (tabular? slashed zero?), with the source and license.
- **Marks.** Five to eight rough sketches. Idea sketches only.
- **Real screen.** The product's hardest screen (the densest table, the chart page, or the front door) redrawn in the direction, the same screen on every board. The move reaches the components, chart and table included: values alone barely change a UI-rich screen. Structure, data, and layout stay put; everything visual may change.
- **Defaults kept.** Each listed default it uses (see `tells.md`) and each move from "Done before" in `preferences.md`, with a one-line reason from the brief.
- **Principal risk.** What would make it fail.

## Scoring before you recommend

Both rounds. Score each 1 to 5 on a weighted table, then recommend one with a runner-up and the reason each sits where it does.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Fits the brand idea and the audience | 20 |
| Distinctiveness against the competitor set | 20 |
| Craft against its own reference | 15 |
| Works in the working product (density, data, states) | 20 |
| Credibility for the category | 10 |
| Memorability (could you sketch it from memory) | 10 |
| Extensibility (marketing, icons, motion, voice) | 5 |

A direction that scores well but fails "works in the working product" is a marketing brand and loses. One that's clearly worse than the reference it borrowed from isn't ready to show.

## Picking and hybrids

The user picks the idea, not the execution; execution gets fixed in the sprints. A hybrid is allowed only when the agent can write one line: "A's idea and voice with C's color and symbol, because both express X." Then it's redrawn as one board and gets the pre-show pass again. Without an honest X it's a blend, and blends read as nobody's idea; say so and ask the user to pick. A preference that's only about color ("A, but C's colors") isn't a hybrid: pick A and carry C's palette into the color sprint. Never pick for the user.

## What the boards show

Round 1: the cards in one grid, shuffled across labels so safe ones don't cluster. Round 2: one artboard per pick, same layout on every board: name and mechanism sentence at the top, the references, the palette as swatches with roles, the type at three sizes with real product copy and a column of real numbers, the mark sketches in a row, the wordmark set in the candidate face, and the real screen at full width. A full board without its real screen isn't shown.
