# Essays and personal posts

The full-voice register. Everything in SKILL.md still applies; this adds what makes a post feel like Conor rather than a well-edited stranger.

## How he opens

Never with a thesis. Always with a moment, situation, or feeling. The reader is dropped into his headspace before they know where the piece is going.

> "I've been spending a good bit of time in the Product Manager interview cycle lately..."
> "Today I turned 29 and I can't help but notice that this birthday isn't hitting quite as hard."
> "It's hard asking for help."
> "Sometimes when I'm on the internet, jumping from hyperlink to hyperlink..."

## How he closes

Quick and soft. No grand summation. Once the point is made, he stops.

> "Feels good to be writing again. Godspeed, folks."
> "More to come!"
> "See you next year."
> "It's not clean, and it's not perfect, but this is what I've found to work."

## Paragraph flow

- Most paragraphs are 3 to 5 sentences. Frequent 1 or 2 sentence paragraphs for emphasis or transition. Never a wall of text.
- Paragraphs open one of five ways: continue the thread ("This is the most obvious point, but worth calling out"), pivot ("But here's the kicker:"), get personal ("Personally, I think of sports here"), ground in experience ("When I started my first job..."), or ask a question ("So what's next for me?").
- A section moves in an arc: set up the idea, get specific (an example, a personal story, someone else's quote), land the takeaway in a short line.
- Transitions are mostly implicit. When explicit, they're casual: "So," "This is why," "Put all of these together," "On the other side of the spectrum."
- Forward momentum. He doesn't circle back or restate.

## The vulnerability lever

His most distinctive move: honest, unguarded lines a more cautious writer would edit out.

> "I don't quite have an answer for why."
> "Nobody really knows."
> "I was unable to be a 'force for progress' since I lacked understanding."
> "I'm undoubtedly privileged to say that so nonchalantly."

These are punctuation, not the whole post. One or two per piece. They earn trust.

## Signature phrases

Reach for these naturally, don't stuff them in.

- "Turns out," / "In short," / "Said another way:" / "Put all of these together,"
- "This makes sense." / "That said," / "Regardless,"
- "In my humble opinion" / "All things considered,"
- "Let's dig in" / "Bear with me" / "This is the part where..."
- "I would be remiss if I didn't..." / "More to come!"
- "I've been kicking around this notion of" / "It isn't a new thing by any means"
- "whether that's [X] or not, time will tell"
- Comfortable with cliché when it serves speed: "a long road ahead," "off and running," "hands dirty"

## Formatting in posts

- Prose is the default. Lists are the exception, and when used, items are 1 to 2 full sentences.
- 0 to 3 subheadings in a typical post, simple and descriptive.
- Bold only for a key term on first introduction.

## Profanity

Rare but real. Maybe once every 10 to 15 posts, for genuine emphasis when nothing else lands as hard: "I really think this speech is fucking awesome, to put it eloquently." Never gratuitous.

## Recurring formats on the site

- **Reading notes** ("Notes on [Book]"): what the book argues, the two or three ideas that stuck, where he disagrees, and what he's doing differently because of it. Quotes do real work; no chapter-by-chapter summary.
- **Principles lists** ("30 Principles for 30 Years"): each principle is one line plus a short paragraph of why, grounded in a specific experience. No preamble about what principles are.
- **Product thinking**: starts from a concrete observation about a tool or interface, builds one mental model, and stops. Doesn't try to be a framework.
